TOMOSHIBI / Market and Business Model / ETHGlobal Mumbai 2026

Who buys “approval” in an era where agents are handed the keys

There are three wallets. The defending side (machine identity and secrets management) is about $24B in 2030; the showing-trust side (makers' marketing budget) about $3B; the loss-shifting side (cyber insurance) $16B a year. Together, $43B. The agent payments flowing above them reach $1.5T in 2030 (188× that of 2026). TOMOSHIBI stands between the two, at the layer where a person presses once. We examined whether a price tag fits here, down to unit cost.

Executive summary

  1. TAM $43B (per year). Defend $24B + show trust $3B + shift loss $16B. TOMOSHIBI reaches both the seller's wallet and the buyer's wallet with one and the same mechanism—the flame is a signboard (marketing) to the maker, and fewer accidents (risk reduction) to the holder.
  2. SAM is $2–7B. What settles it is our own measurement. 0G judges on “does money move”, not on “how much”—$4,800 or $1, both are “decisive”. So nearly every agent payment is subject to approval, and SAM grows at the same speed as the agent economy.
  3. Three revenue lines. In one of them, the motive to keep paying is built into how the product works. Raise the version and the flames all go out at once—so the skill's maker buys trust again with every release. No anti-churn feature needs to be added.
  4. Measured cost is $0.000036 (¥0.0056) per judgement. Both the token counts and the unit prices came from production 0G. Even used up to the ceiling of the $9/month plan, gross margin is 80%, and the free tier of 2,000 judgements costs $0.07 a month. Cost is not a constraint on pricing—so the price can only be justified by value to the buyer, not by cost. That is what we check next.

01Which market are we in

None of the three neighbouring markets. The layer that takes a human approval one call at a time, without holding the key, has no price tag yet.

The relation to the neighbours is not competition; we sit on top of them. Secrets management answers “where the key is kept”; TOMOSHIBI answers “whether that key may be used right now”. Both are needed, and nobody sells the latter yet.

Why we may count this as market. Because what both wallets are buying is the same thing: trust. The selling side pays to show it, the buying side pays to lose less. But we do not count the whole amount as moving to us. In SAM we place only $0.17B for flame display (500k makers × $348/year) and $0.8B for accident transfer (the 5% of premiums caused by agents). Inflating the order of magnitude stops at how we read TAM.

This is a hypothesis, and how to test it is already decided. Whether P2 pays it as marketing spend is measured by the difference in adoption between a version with a flame and a version without one. If no difference shows up, B (flame display, $29/month) does not stand—measure that difference before cutting the price.

The 3% figure is what matters. The accidents have not all happened yet. Go to sell the “stopping layer” after accidents are routine, and by then each payment company will have built a thin approval feature of its own. What is needed now is to put out the pattern from the side that does not hold the key.

Write the headwind, not just the tailwind. “Making a person press” is heavy as an experience. The competitor is not another company; it is the ease of letting everything through automatically. That is why TOMOSHIBI is designed to show nothing most of the time (the pricing in §04 is matched to that—we do not charge for the silent hours).

05Business model

Put the price tag on the judgement, not on volume. Not holding the key is the product, so the path of earning by holding it is cut off from the start.

What we will not do (it would contradict the product)

  • Never hold keys or funds. Holding them means insurance and regulation, and the very claim “you do not have to hand it over” disappears
  • Take no percentage of payment volume. The moment volume becomes revenue, we become a company that loses by stopping things. We would become the headwind in §03 ourselves
  • Never sell request contents, never train on them. The contents sit inside the 0G seal, and we cannot read them either. Not being able to read them is what we sell
  • Never make the flame purchasable. A star you can buy is cold. Allow even one flame lit with money and the product's value goes to zero

Per judgement, 300 tokens and $0.000036 (¥0.0056). The 10× range has collapsed—five times cheaper even than the lower bound we had guessed. Cost is not a constraint on pricing.

Two levers work. One is not sending everyday requests to 0G—reads (GET) inside the scope pass on rules alone, and only requests that could move money are handed to 0G. In our measurements only the billing-related ones come out “decisive”, so the number of judgements drops by an order of magnitude. The other is caching, which already hits 63% of input.

But because cost collapsed, the purpose of those two levers changed. Halving $0.000036 helps nobody. The reason to send fewer is no longer cost but speed alone (not making anyone wait 2 seconds). There is no longer any reason to cut safety for the sake of cost.

The moment we decide not to send, a path of “passed without reading” comes into existence. How far rules alone may pass is a trade between safety and cost, and must not be decided quietly. The condition is that a request passed on rules is also recorded in the trail as “passed without asking 0G”.
